Two paths, one call

A forecast-driven session layer that keeps voice and control traffic alive while a device moves between Wi-Fi, 5G, satellite, and wired networks. The name is Arabic for “two paths”.

EDGE challenge: seamless handover across heterogeneous networks

Executive summary

Devices today change networks after the active link has failed. In our model, a call then goes silent for an assumed 1.6 seconds when a wireless network stays down for 1.0 s or longer. Masarain forecasts the failure from signals a device can observe, sends a second copy of each critical packet over the backup network only during the risky seconds, and caps the extra data with a budget. We compared it with six other mechanisms in a packet-level simulator and built it as an encrypted prototype, which runs live over emulated links in our demo.

1.6 s

about

Silence lasts about 1.6 seconds when the link stays down for 1.0 s or longer, and less after a shorter drop. Both times are assumptions; the sensitivity runs vary the detection time.

Design question: can a device get the reliability of sending everything twice while paying for only a small share of it, using signals it can observe?

Why it matters

Wi-Fi, 5G, satellite, and wired links fail in different ways when a user moves: coverage fades, cells hand over, Starlink satellite links reconfigure every 15 seconds, and cables are unplugged.

A device normally keeps one network active and changes only after that network has failed. In our simulation this loses 5.3% of voice packets, causes 2.8 audible gaps per minute, and degrades the audio in 14.7% of all seconds.

The traffic at stake is small but critical: a voice call and a control or telemetry stream. For a field team or an unmanned vehicle, a silence of 1.6 seconds is a missed order or a lost control link.

Why a budget

A token bucket limits copies to a fixed long-run share of the critical traffic. It keeps the cost of copies on a metered backup link predictable, and bounds the copies sent when a forecast is wrong or when someone feeds the device false signals.

The forecaster uses only what a real device can observe. In the simulator it never reads the true link state, and its signal readings carry a calibration error.

Security

  • Every packet is encrypted and authenticated, and the server rejects replays and stale timestamps.
  • The client accepts an acknowledgment only for its own session, path, and send time.
  • The budget caps how much traffic a forged risk signal could make the device send.

Deployability

  • Runs in user space on a stock Windows laptop and is built for any two IP links; no operator, router, or kernel support is required, only a reachable server.
  • Python 3.8 with the standard library and the cryptography package for the cipher; on a real adapter the Wi-Fi signal is read from the Windows WLAN interface about ten times per second.

Simulator

Packet-level engine on a 10 ms link grid, written for this project.

Wi-Fi: log-distance path loss, correlated shadowing, roaming, and association loss.

5G: signal-triggered handovers with a short interruption and occasional failures; coverage holes on the rural road.

Satellite, modeled on Starlink: a delay spike at each 15 s reconfiguration [9, 10], plus random obstructions.

Every parameter is tagged in the code as taken from literature, to be measured, or assumed.

Ablation, cost-curve, and sensitivity runs test each part of the design and the main assumed parameters.

The forecaster is also tested on its own: we count how many real disruptions it flags in advance, how early, and how many of its alarms are false.

Prototype over emulated links

The prototype is real software: a client and a server with an encrypted packet format, real timers, and the same policy module as the simulator.

A relay between them replays the simulator’s link traces: it drops and delays each packet as the trace says and reports the Wi-Fi signal the device would measure.

Two clients run at once through the same relay, so an existing mechanism and Masarain meet identical conditions; a live page shows both calls side by side.

Running five of the eight mechanisms this way checks the simulator against real sockets and scheduling.

No radio measurement was made: tests on real Wi-Fi and 5G are prepared (scripts and protocol) and are the next step.

Guidelines for quality of service and quality of experience, and the limits of this work

PROTECT THE FLOW, NOT THE LINK

Six guidelines for seamless handover of critical traffic

1Protect the flow, not the link.

Classify traffic by deadline and copy only the flows that cannot wait; leave bulk traffic on one path.

2Act before the loss when a signal exists.

Signal trends give seconds of warning, and the satellite clock is known in advance; reacting after loss gives up the first burst.

3Put a budget on redundancy, and price each copy against it.

State the share of traffic that may be copied, and raise the risk required for a copy as the budget drains, so cost and exposure to false signals stay bounded.

4Escalate in steps.

Copy first, move the primary only when risk persists, and return only after the preferred link has been healthy for several seconds.

5Keep a reactive floor, and add error correction on a single path.

Unpredictable failures need protection within a few packets of the first loss; when one path is left, parity repairs random loss that copies cannot.

6Measure experience, not only loss.

Report gaps and voice quality in the worst seconds of a call; an average hides the moments users notice.

LIMITS OF THIS WORK

  • The link models are synthetic. Many parameters are assumptions, marked in the code; the sensitivity runs vary the main ones. Replay of recorded 5G and satellite traces is the next step.
  • A fixed signal threshold inside the same ladder already captures most of the gain; the forecast adds a smaller margin (slide 7).
  • The 5G forecast assumes access to the modem’s signal readings. A tethered laptop cannot see them, so the prototype forecasts Wi-Fi only and reacts on 5G.
  • No satellite terminal was available, so the satellite results are simulation only.
  • Copies need a second network that is already connected, which costs standby energy and data.
  • The budget caps copies only: when rung 3 moves the call, the backup network carries all of it. Averaged over the scenarios, Masarain sends 55% of one copy of the call on the backup path, probes included; full redundancy sends 98%.
  • Only two paths and two small flows were studied; many users sharing a link were not.
  • The prototype ran over emulated links only. Real Wi-Fi and 5G will add effects the model lacks.

From a laptop prototype to a gateway feature: a plan for real-life use

NOW End-host prototype
MENTORING PHASE Real links, then a gateway
LATER Standards-aligned product

An application-level session layer on a laptop: two sockets, an encrypted packet format, and the ladder policy, demonstrated live over emulated links. It needs nothing from the network operator, only a server both links can reach.

Field tests on real Wi-Fi and 5G with the prepared protocol, then the same policy on a vehicle or base gateway that protects the voice and control traffic of every device behind it.

The trigger and budget logic fits the redundant steering mode of 3GPP ATSSS and the schedulers of multipath QUIC and MP-DCCP, which leave the choice of when to copy to the implementation.

What we would do with EDGE’s mentors

  1. Replace the synthetic links with recorded traces and with EDGE’s own link profiles.
  2. Run the prepared field protocol on real Wi-Fi and 5G, and calibrate the forecaster on the device.
  1. Add the modem’s signal readings through a phone application, so 5G is forecast on the device as well.
  2. Test on a moving vehicle with a satellite terminal as the preferred path.
