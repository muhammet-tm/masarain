Reference materials

This page holds the material behind the Masarain entry for the EDGE challenge of Advanced Technology Pioneers 2026.

The team submitted the application on 11 October 2026. On 4 November 2026, after a review by an industry mentor, the team set the link models from published measurements, defined six traffic classes that run alone and a model of TCP, added jitter and the time between packets to the measures of every run, and ran the simulations again. The tables on this page give the results of 4 November 2026, so several figures differ from those of the submitted deck and video. The one exception is the comparison of the prototype with the simulator, which the team made on 8 October 2026 and did not repeat. The records of the project keep the results of 8 October 2026.

Results by scenario

Eight mechanisms ran on the same link traces in six scenarios, with 30 random seeds per scenario. In the first table, each figure is a mean over the 30 runs of a scenario, and its first block weights the six scenarios equally.

Every result in this section comes from the packet-level simulator or from the prototype over emulated links, and none comes from a real radio network. The one real Wi-Fi measurement of the project appears with the plan for the test on real networks. The link models follow published measurements, but they remain models: the results in this section replay no recorded trace, and the simulator does not model contention and queueing on a loaded Wi-Fi network. The limits of this work are listed on the overview page.

Definitions of the measures

Voice packets lost or late
The share of voice packets that did not arrive within 80 ms of sending. A late packet is as useless to the listener as a lost one, so the measure counts the two together.
Seconds with degraded audio
The share of the seconds of a call in which 5% or more of the voice packets were lost or late.
Gaps
Runs of consecutive lost or late voice packets that last 200 ms or more, counted per minute of the call.
Jitter (RFC 3550)
The interarrival jitter of RFC 3550, averaged over the call. For each voice packet that arrives, the change in its delay against the packet before it enters a running mean with a gain of 1/16. Lost packets do not enter it.
Time between voice packets
The time from one voice packet to the next at the receiver, at the 99.9th percentile of each run. The call sends a packet every 20 ms, and a lost packet or an outage lengthens this time, so it shows the silences that jitter does not show.
Telemetry deadline misses
The share of telemetry messages that did not arrive within 250 ms of sending.
Extra data sent as copies
The bytes of second copies and parity packets, as a percentage of the bytes of one copy of the voice and telemetry packets. It does not include probes.
Keepalive probes
The bytes of the keepalive probes on the idle path, on the same scale.
All data on the backup network
Every byte sent on the backup network (the moved call, the second copies, and the probes), on the same scale. The budget caps copies only: when the third rung of the ladder moves the call, the backup network carries all of it.
95% interval
A t-interval over the 30 seeds of a scenario. For the block that covers all scenarios, the six scenarios are averaged for each seed first.

Multipath, lowest RTT sends each packet on the path with the lowest round-trip time (RTT) and sends no copies. Perfect foresight is a bound for comparison. It copies exactly the packets that would otherwise be lost or late. In the T4 base scenario, Masarain and signal-threshold duplication give identical figures. Both use the same ladder and budget and differ only in the trigger, and a pulled cable gives neither trigger a warning. The budget of Masarain is 20% of the traffic with a deadline in the long run. The bucket starts full, so a run of a few minutes can exceed it.

Definitions of the forecaster terms

Event
A disruption of the simulated link itself, which the forecaster never reads.
Flagged in advance
An alarm was on at the start of the event or within the 1.5 s before it.
Warning
The time from the start of that alarm to the start of the event.
Onset of loss
The first moment at which the path is down or its true loss probability reaches the threshold, after at least one second without either.
Alarm episode
A run of alarm samples; samples no more than 0.3 s apart form one episode. An episode counts as followed by degradation when the path is down, or its true loss probability is at or above the threshold, at some moment from the start of the episode to 1.5 s after its end, or when an event is in progress at the start of the episode.
Time degraded
The share of time in which the path was down or its true loss probability was at or above the threshold.

The satellite-obstruction row does not show a forecast. Its 12% of flagged events coincide with alarms for a scheduled reconfiguration, and chance alone gives about 12%. A pulled cable gives no warning at all.

Early warning has a price: 55% of Wi-Fi alarms and 44% of 5G alarms are not followed by real degradation. The budget keeps those false alarms affordable.

The prototype against the simulator

The prototype is real software: a client and a server with an encrypted packet format, real timers, and the same policy module as the simulator. A relay between them replays the simulator's link trace. It implements five of the eight mechanisms. A tethered laptop cannot see the signal readings of the 5G modem, so the prototype forecasts Wi-Fi only and reacts on 5G. The simulator runs of this comparison are set the same way: they forecast Wi-Fi only, and their signal readings carry no calibration error. The comparison covers one trace of 298 s on the campus walk, so it checks the software against the simulator and says nothing about real radio links.

The team made the prototype runs and the simulator runs beside them on 8 October 2026, on the link models of that day, and did not repeat them with the link models of 4 November 2026. The simulator column of this table therefore does not come from the simulator of the other tables on this page.

Quality of service and of experience

The tables use two kinds of measure. The first describes what the network delivers, and the second describes what the user notices. The simulator reports both for every run.

Quality of service (QoS)
What the network delivers for each packet: its delay, its jitter, the time between arrivals, and whether it arrives at all.
Quality of experience (QoE)
What the user notices: the score of a call in the E-model of ITU-T G.107 (reference 11), the frozen frames of a video call, the stalls of a streaming player, which video services such as YouTube and Netflix count as rebuffering, and the pauses of a bulk transfer. Each traffic class is scored by its own measure.
Jitter (RFC 3550)
The variation of the delay from one arriving packet to the next, smoothed as RFC 3550 prescribes for RTP receivers. A packet that never arrives does not enter it.
Time between packets
The inter-packet arrival time (IPAT): the time from one packet to the next at the receiver. A loss or an outage shows up as a long time between packets. The tables give its high percentiles and its longest value in each run.
Times longer than three packet intervals
The number of times per minute that the time between packets exceeds three packet intervals, which for the voice call is 60 ms.
Delay variation (PDV)
The 99.9th percentile of the one-way delay minus its minimum, in the form of ITU-T Y.1541. It appears in the report of each network alone.

Over all scenarios, Masarain shows more jitter than switch after failure (5.01 against 4.81 ms) and a shorter 99.9th percentile of the time between voice packets (51.9 against 228.9 ms). Jitter counts only the packets that arrive, so a mechanism that saves packets over a second network with a different delay can show more jitter while it leaves fewer silences. This page therefore reports the two measures together.

Traffic classes

Masarain reads what each flow carries and which transport it uses. Only a class whose packets lose their value after a deadline gets copies on the second network. Masarain can move traffic without a deadline, here streaming video and bulk data over TCP, to the other network on the third rung of the ladder, but it never copies that traffic.

Three of the six classes run over TCP. The simulator models a TCP flow inside the session with a retransmission timeout of at least 200 ms, which doubles after each retransmission that fails, up to a cap of 1.6 s that the model assumes, and with in-order delivery. Over TCP, a lost segment therefore also delays every segment behind it.

Data files

Four files hold the figures of the tables on this page in a form that a spreadsheet or a script can read. The ablation file also gives each scenario, which the table above does not, and the file of the traffic classes also gives each class by the network that fails. All are plain comma-separated text with one header row.

  • masarain_main_comparison.csv56 rows: every mechanism in every scenario, and the means over all scenarios. Each of the nine measures is followed by its interval, and the last column gives all data on the backup network.
  • masarain_ablation.csv63 rows: the nine variants of Masarain in every scenario and over all scenarios, with the same measures and the paired change in voice loss against the full design. The two change columns are empty for the full design, which is the reference.
  • masarain_traffic_classes.csv240 rows: each traffic class alone, with every mechanism, over all scenarios and by the network that fails. The columns of the measures of experience are empty for the classes that they do not apply to.
  • masarain_network_benchmark.csv48 rows: each network alone in every scenario that uses it, and the mechanisms filed by the network that fails. The first part fills the columns of the link measures and the second part those of the voice call.

The names of the scenarios, mechanisms, classes, networks, and measures are those of this page. In the first two files, each interval column gives the half-width of the 95% interval of the column before it. The last two files give means with two decimals and no intervals.

Method in brief

Simulator
The simulator is a packet-level engine written for this project. It replays six mobility scenarios on a 10 ms link grid. The Wi-Fi, 5G, satellite, and wired links each have their own model, with parameters taken from published measurements and standards wherever the code cites a source.
Traffic
The main runs carry a G.711 voice call of 50 packets per second and a telemetry stream of 10 messages per second. The six traffic classes also ran one at a time.
Paired runs
Every mechanism meets the same link traces and the same random draws for loss and jitter in each run, so a difference between two mechanisms comes from the mechanisms.
Statistics
Each scenario runs with 30 random seeds. Results are means with 95% intervals over the seeds, and comparisons are paired differences on the same seeds. The one choice tuned on data, the price of a copy as the budget drains, was made on separate development seeds.
Forecaster
The forecaster reads only what a device can observe. In the simulator it never reads the true link state, and its signal readings carry a calibration error.
Escalation ladder
The escalation ladder is one module, and the simulator and the prototype client both import it.
Network layers
Masarain is a session-layer overlay over UDP, between the applications and the transport layer. Multipath TCP and multipath QUIC work at the transport layer. The Access Traffic Steering, Switching and Splitting (ATSSS) of 3GPP works in the 5G device and the core network, and a software-defined wide-area network (SD-WAN) is a network-layer overlay on an edge router.

Plan for the test on real networks

The team has not yet run this test of the prototype on two real networks. It used emulated links for the submission and prepared the plan below as the next step. One real measurement exists so far: a single Wi-Fi drop, reported first.

First real measurement: one Wi-Fi drop from a Linux virtual machine

A CyberOps Workstation virtual machine (Linux) in VirtualBox, bridged to the laptop's Wi-Fi card, sent an ICMP echo to the access point (a phone hotspot) about every 22 ms while a team member carried the laptop out of coverage. Over a window of 40 s, the probe measured a median round-trip time of 3.8 ms, an RFC 3550 jitter of 3.8 ms computed on half the round trip, and one outage of 24.4 s. The simulator then replayed the measured link through the same engine and policy code, with its own 5G model as the backup, over ten random draws. Switch after failure lost 5.35% of the voice, Masarain 0.37% for 4% extra data, and full redundancy 0.28% for 38%. Signal-threshold duplication tied with Masarain on this trace, so the forecast gave no gain over a fixed threshold here. This is one drop on one network: the backup is modeled, the team measured no real 5G link, and the prototype did not take part, so the test does not replace the plan below. The procedure is written down in VM_WIFI_TEST.md, which comes with the source code.

Purpose
The test has two purposes: to show that the prototype runs on real hardware, and to measure the curve from Wi-Fi signal to packet loss for the test laptop. The measured curve replaces the assumed curve in the forecaster.
Setup
Wi-Fi from a phone that serves as a hotspot at a fixed spot is the preferred path, and 5G through a second phone tethered by USB is the backup path. A Windows laptop runs the client. A Linux server with a public address, which both networks can reach, runs the server. Where possible the two phones use different operators, so that the two paths do not share one cell tower.
Checks
These checks must pass before any measurement. Both network interfaces stay up together and reach the server. The Wi-Fi signal reader delivers about 50 or more readings in 5 seconds, because the forecaster needs about 10 readings per second. The self-test of every mechanism passes. The clock of the laptop is correct, because the server drops packets whose timestamp is more than 120 seconds from its own clock. A smoke test of 30 seconds shows both paths at the client, no forged or stale packets at the server, and about 50 voice packets per second.
Calibration
The calibration consists of three walks of 180 seconds that leave Wi-Fi coverage and return. They measure the loss at each signal level and the signal at which the adapter gives up the link.
Measurement
The measurement consists of 85 runs of 120 seconds with the five mechanisms that the prototype implements: switch after failure; multipath, lowest RTT; reactive duplication; full redundancy; and Masarain. The order of the mechanisms rotates from one block of runs to the next, as in a Latin square, so that no mechanism always runs early or always runs late in a session.

Analysis
The analysis reports, for each run, the voice packets lost or late, the telemetry deadline misses, the longest gap, and the extra data sent as copies. The clocks of the laptop and the server are not synchronized, so a packet counts as late in relation to the fastest delivery of its run.
Pass conditions
The plan sets pass conditions for the checks and none for the results. With four to eight runs per mechanism and test, the samples are small. The team will therefore report the results as a feasibility check beside the simulation, and the simulation remains the main evidence.
Exclusions
The team deletes no run. It notes unusual events for each run, decides every exclusion afterward, and reports it.
Scope
The plan covers walking only. The drive scenarios stay in the simulator.
